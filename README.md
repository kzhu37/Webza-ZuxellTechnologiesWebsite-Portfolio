## From idea to real client

Webza began as a web-design agency concept. We wanted to build custom websites around the needs of individual businesses instead of creating another project where we controlled every requirement ourselves.

That changed the problem immediately. We compiled local businesses, divided outreach across the team, and dealt with repeated rejection or no response. Finding the problem became part of the project. We had to explain what we could offer, sound credible, and convince somebody to trust a student team before we could even begin client work.

In early March, I connected the team with **Zuxell Technologies through a family connection**. Zuxell worked in optical engineering and needed a stronger online presence. The retained requirements note gave us a concrete five-part information structure:

```text
Home
About Us
Expertise
Services
Contact Us
```

It also pointed us toward an established optics-industry site as a reference. From then on, the design had external constraints. We had to learn enough about an unfamiliar technical business to decide what visitors should see first, what tone would create credibility, and how much visual personality was appropriate.

## Two products, two audiences

The project produced two related websites, but they were intentionally not designed to look alike.

| | **Zuxell client site** | **Webza agency site** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Audience** | Engineering clients and technical visitors | Businesses looking for web-design help |
| **Goal** | Establish clarity and technical credibility | Show personality, capability, and service positioning |
| **Visual direction** | Restrained, precise, professional | Bold, expressive, pitch-driven |
| **Content focus** | Optical-engineering services and inquiry | Agency identity, services, team, and process |
| **Role in the project** | External client deliverable | Our own storefront and service showcase |

The Zuxell direction centered the three service areas preserved in the client materials: **laser manufacturing, lens design, and optical testing**. The landing page was treated as the key first impression, so the client site emphasized clarity, trust, and technical restraint rather than visual effects for their own sake.

Webza could be more promotional because the agency itself was the product being marketed. Its spring site presented service ideas around **design, speed, mobile responsiveness, security, strategy, and support**. The presentation materials also explored an initial website build plus ongoing maintenance as a possible service model.

## Feedback-driven iteration

The first complete Zuxell version was ready before March Break and was deliberately treated as a first version. After the break, we returned to the client site, reviewed feedback, clarified sections, polished design choices, and strengthened the landing-page experience while also building Webza's own site.

The retained Git history shows that this was more than a wording pass. Between the **March 9** first complete checkpoint and the **April 3** revision checkpoint:

| Source evidence | March 9 checkpoint | April 3 revision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Structure** | Most structure, styling, and interaction in one `index.html` | Structure, styling, and interaction separated across `index.html`, `style.css`, and `script.js` |
| **File size** | `index.html`: about 87 KB | `index.html`: about 52 KB, `style.css`: about 49 KB, `script.js`: about 8 KB |
| **Recorded change** | First complete client interface | 1,516 changed lines in `index.html`, plus a new 911-line stylesheet and 245-line interaction script |
| **Interaction direction** | Complete interface in a tightly coupled implementation | Expanded navigation, motion, reveal, counter, card, parallax, and progress behaviors |

The revision also exposed a design trade-off. The April interaction layer experimented with an optics-themed entrance, rotating headline text, animated counters, card tilt, parallax, progress indication, and staggered reveals. Those features proved that the team could build a richer interaction layer, but they also made a later lesson visible: **more interaction is not automatically better interaction.**

| Observation or constraint | Decision | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A fictional client would have been easier but less meaningful | Keep pursuing a real organization despite unsuccessful outreach | The project gained external requirements and a real stakeholder context |
| The first client build was useful but still provisional | Return to it after March Break instead of treating completion as final | Sections became clearer and the landing-page direction was refined |
| Zuxell and Webza served different audiences | Give each product its own visual identity and content priorities | Zuxell stayed restrained while Webza became more promotional |
| The early client implementation was tightly coupled | Separate structure, styling, and interaction during revision | The codebase became easier to reason about and continue refining |
| Team availability changed after March Break | Reprioritize work and clarify handoffs | The client site, agency site, and final pitch continued moving |

## Technical evolution

### Original spring implementation

The spring project used browser fundamentals rather than a front-end framework. The March 9 build concentrated the interface in a single large HTML file. By April 3, the revised client site used focused files for structure, styling, and interaction:

```text
index.html  -> structure and content
style.css   -> visual system and responsive layout
script.js   -> navigation and interaction
```

The April JavaScript layer included an optics-themed entrance, tab navigation, mobile navigation, contact interaction, scroll reveals, navbar scroll behavior, rotating text, animated counters, card tilt, parallax movement, a progress indicator, and staggered reveals. The implementation is not presented as advanced framework engineering. Its value is that the team iterated a complete client interface, adapted it to an external audience, and learned to distinguish purposeful interaction from decoration.

### Current public reconstruction

The current public Zuxell site keeps the original lightweight HTML, CSS, and JavaScript direction while making the showcase safer and easier to evaluate. It adds:

- semantic section structure and hash-based navigation;
- responsive desktop, tablet, mobile, and narrow-mobile layouts;
- keyboard-operable navigation with mobile-menu focus trapping and focus restoration;
- visible focus states and reduced-motion support;
- a browser-only inquiry demonstration that validates entries without transmitting data;
- dependency-free local serving and automated content, server, and browser smoke checks.

The current `script.js` is intentionally small. It manages section routing, navigation state, keyboard behavior, focus handling, inquiry validation, and the current year without adding a framework or runtime dependency.

### Verification

`npm test` combines syntax checks with content, server, and browser smoke tests. The browser path checks desktop, laptop, tablet, mobile, and narrow-mobile layouts; hash navigation; mobile-menu accessibility state; focus restoration; inquiry validation; image alternative text; placeholder links; metadata; and console errors.

The repository also audits public text for long dash characters so README, documentation, source comments, and interface copy stay consistent with this portfolio's writing standard.

## Lessons and next steps

**Formalize client onboarding earlier.** We had a requirements note, but a stronger engagement would define scope, content ownership, revision expectations, and a feedback schedule before development accelerated.

**Define ownership and handoffs earlier.** Team availability changed during the project. Clearer responsibility boundaries, earlier check-ins, and a shared task tracker would have reduced coordination friction.

**Preserve evidence while building.** The source history survived, but an untouched before-and-after visual record did not. A stronger process would preserve dated screenshots, feedback notes, and revision decisions alongside the code.

**Treat communication as part of engineering.** The final startup-style pitch forced us to connect client acquisition, design choices, implementation, challenges, revision, and business framing into one understandable story. Doing that continuously would have improved both the work and its documentation.

The larger lesson was that software becomes more demanding when the constraints come from real people. Technical implementation still matters, but so do trust, scope, communication, audience, revision, and the ability to explain why a decision exists.
